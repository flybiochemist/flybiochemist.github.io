Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, first and second among neurodegenerative diseases, are easy to lump together, but they differ as much as they overlap.

Take dementia. The question is whether memory once lost can be recovered — and even if you make new cells to fill in for the dead ones, there is no telling whether memory comes back. Whether memory takes the form of protein or of neural circuitry, or whether glia need to be included too, is complicated. But at minimum, filling the gaps left by dead cells with stem cells may halt the decline while falling well short of restoring the way things used to be.

## For dopamine, restoring the supply is enough

Parkinson's, though, is a problem caused by falling dopamine release as the dopaminergic neurons die. Ask whether it is solved by restoring release, and the answer is broadly yes. Putting in dopaminergic neurons, or simply less-differentiated stem cells, leaves room for treatment as long as more dopamine gets released. So there is more scope for stem cell therapies in Parkinson's than in Alzheimer's.

In that respect Parkinson's resembles type 1 diabetes. Even more so given that stem-cell-derived dopamine- or insulin-producing cells can be placed not in their original location but in another related tissue and still give a therapeutic effect through increased secretion.

In Parkinson's, think of the dopamine-releasing cells as having their heads in the substantia nigra and their hands and feet in the striatum. The voluntary movement you perform when you intend something is regulated through the dopaminergic circuitry of this nigrostriatal route, which is why a shortage of dopamine leaves muscles rigid and the amplitude of movement hard to control.

So in Parkinson's, stem cells are increasingly placed straight into the striatum, which is larger and can be injected without error, where tissue loss is not yet advanced so the transplanted cells are under less stress, and where the dopamine needs to be distributed anyway.

## Even a stopgap is a cure if it lasts 20 or 30 years

There is of course the objection that adding stem cells while the brain environment keeps deteriorating is like urinating on a frozen foot, and it may well be a stopgap. But people who work on neurodegeneration always say the same thing, as with HIV suppressants: you only need to postpone progression until the person dies of something else. For a disease that starts after 60, a stopgap lasting 20 or 30 years is already a cure, surely.

I do work on Parkinson's, but I keep hoping some single method will become a mainstream treatment, make my research redundant, and let me move to another disease. Stem cells still have a long way to go, though, and someone still has to find the various ways to clean up the house the stem cells will move into, and the drug targets to realise them. So it looks like I will stay busy.

We learn in biology class that a red blood cell hangs four oxygen molecules on hemoglobin, drops them where oxygen is scarce, and returns loaded with carbon dioxide. So nobody paid much attention to what hemoglobin might do outside red blood cells.

But from the mid-to-late 2000s, papers began reporting hemoglobin present in the brains of patients with brain disease. Naturally, the assumption at first was simply that disease meant brain hemorrhage, and hemorrhage meant hemoglobin would be there. There were also reports that introducing red blood cells or hemoglobin activated immune-related cells in the brain. So there has been a steady trickle of papers wondering whether this hemoglobin has some connection to neurodegeneration.

## Hemoglobin the brain makes for itself

Recently, though, researchers at KIST and IBS reported that this hemoglobin is not coming from blood at all — it naturally exists in neurons and glia — and that it functions as an enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide, the main culprit in intracellular oxidation.

So would boosting this antioxidant activity of hemoglobin help treat neurodegenerative disease and slow brain ageing? The team made a brain-penetrant compound that boosts the hydrogen-peroxide-degrading activity of hemoglobin's beta subunit roughly a hundredfold, and used it to raise antioxidant activity in the brain.

This antioxidant effect works in neurons too, but more strongly in astrocytes, the cells that support them. It returned the overactivated form seen in disease back to its normal function, and protected mouse models of Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and ALS alike, even extending mouse lifespan. At that point it starts to sound like a cure-all.

Hemoglobin, which we thought lived only in red blood cells, turns out to carry an antioxidant enzyme function we never knew about there. Our bodies still hold plenty of possibilities we have not found yet.

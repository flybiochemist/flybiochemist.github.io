Down syndrome, the most common chromosomal developmental disorder, arises from nondisjunction of chromosome 21 during development, leaving three copies where there should be two. As a result, the several hundred genes on chromosome 21 all show roughly 50% higher expression. Abeta, the key protein fragment driving Alzheimer's, is produced when enzymes act like scissors on the protein APP, and disease begins once the resulting Abeta aggregates — and APP just happens to sit on chromosome 21.

So Down syndrome patients face elevated dementia risk from their forties on. Many of the drugs and antibody therapies currently targeting Abeta in dementia are now in trials in Down syndrome patients specifically. One of them, Levetiracetam, is today's subject.

## Why it worked was unknown

Reports that patients on Levetiracetam had lower odds of developing dementia than those not on it go back to the 2000s, and mouse models had confirmed a benefit too. But how it worked, and how it related to Abeta and tau — Alzheimer's two major culprit proteins — had never been clearly established.

A cell's needed proteins are made in the ER, folded, sent through various finishing steps in the Golgi, and delivered to wherever they work. Some proteins are loaded onto vesicles that carry neurotransmitter for release, and shipped out to the signalling terminal, the synapse. Levetiracetam attaches to this delivery-truck-like vesicle and slows its transport. That is how it dampens the burst release of neurotransmitter in epilepsy and lowers signalling intensity.

As it happens, the very vesicle this drug acts on has long been known as the site where APP gets processed into Abeta.

## Disrupting a crowded space

Isolating just the vesicles Levetiracetam inhibits (SV2a) and analysing them turned up a great deal of APP, the Abeta precursor, along with BACE1 and PSEN1, the scissor enzymes that cut it.

Skipping the long version and going straight to the conclusion: when Levetiracetam keeps SV2a vesicles from being transported smoothly, the excess APP protein that would have gone onto the vesicle instead simply gets sent to the plasma membrane.

The odds of APP meeting its scissor enzymes are wildly different between a small, crowded space packed tight with protein for neurotransmitter transport, and some random spot on the vast plasma membrane — roughly the difference between a blind date between two Koreans in Seoul versus one in the American South. So the chance of APP getting cut into Abeta drops, and the timing is pushed back — which is, apparently, the mechanism by which Levetiracetam works.

Unlikely to become a drug on its own any time soon, but good work.

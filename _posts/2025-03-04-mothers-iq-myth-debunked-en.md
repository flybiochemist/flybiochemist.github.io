I saw a post on Threads one day claiming that "a child's — or specifically a son's — intelligence takes after the mother." The evidence cited was mostly that KBS and various newspapers had reported on it recently, and those broadcasts were citing a 2019 article in The Independent.

That Independent article turns out to be a rehash of 2016-era articles with one additional paper tacked on, and those 2016 articles trace back to a blogger named Jennifer Delgado, who on her blog Psychology Spot had rounded up a few papers through 2012 under the title "your child's intelligence comes from the mother." The core claim: one study tracked 12,686 people aged 14-22 starting in 1994, and found the mother's IQ to be the single best predictor of the child's intelligence.

So how solid are the papers this blogger's claim actually rests on?

## Source paper #1: it was actually a breastfeeding study

Look at the paper's actual title: "Effect of breast feeding on intelligence in children: prospective study, sibling pairs analysis, and meta-analysis."

Right. This is a paper about whether breastfeeding relates to intelligence (measured by IQ) — and it found no such link. The factor that did significantly predict the child's intelligence was the mother's intelligence (the child's IQ tracked within about ±15 of the mother's), and the father's intelligence was never examined, since fathers, understandably, don't breastfeed. The strongest conclusion you could actually draw from this paper is not "intelligence comes from the mother and not the father" — it's that the study never tested the father at all. The moment you cherry-pick data like this to fit a preferred conclusion, that blog's credibility drops to zero.

## Source #2: a 40-year-old mouse paper, applied to humans

The blogger's other piece of evidence involves imprinting patterns on the X chromosome depending on whether it came from mother or father. A person born female has two X chromosomes, twice the gene dosage of someone with one — so cells randomly and alternately silence specific regions on one copy or the other (imprinting) to keep total expression level roughly constant (dosage compensation). In both sexes, certain genes on the autosomes are also silenced by imprinting depending on whether they came from mother or father. The blogger cited one paper claiming the maternal X chromosome is expressed in the cortex (which handles intelligence), while the paternal X is expressed in regions like the hypothalamus, unrelated to intelligence.

But the cited paper is from 1984 — forty years old. And it isn't even in humans; it's a mouse paper. Do mice and humans actually share the same imprinting pattern? According to a 2019 Cell paper summarizing the field, mouse and human imprinted genes overlap far less often than they diverge.

## Source #3: the actual candidate genes aren't even imprinted

So, mouse or human, how many genes that are paternally imprinted (and therefore expressed only from the maternal copy) are actually linked to intelligence? A 2017 Nature paper, using data from 78,037 UK Biobank participants, identified 47 genes associated with intelligence. None of them were imprinted, and none sat on a sex chromosome. Which means proving this claim with a 40-year-old mouse experiment was never possible to begin with.

## Conclusion

Any claim needs at least some minimal supporting evidence, and this blogger's claim that "intelligence passes from mother to child" simply doesn't have it. Present this level of evidence at a conference and you'd get thoroughly taken apart. And yet watching a blog post ride into a respectable newspaper, get reproduced from there, flow all the way into Korea, and become something like common knowledge — even feeding into parenting and humanities lectures — is genuinely bizarre to watch play out. Intelligence does not come from the mother alone. The honest answer is that we simply don't know yet.

For good measure, I checked three more things:

1. Under the X-chromosome theory, is there research on IQ differences between sons and daughters in families where the parents' IQs differ substantially? There wasn't any.
2. Surely the claim isn't literally that the intelligence-related gene on the paternal X happens to be the one silenced by imprinting? That is in fact exactly the mechanism proposed, and it doesn't seem to hold up.
3. The study population: surely they didn't only look at the mother-child correlation and skip the father-child one? They really did skip it.

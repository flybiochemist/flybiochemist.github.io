Writing this up now that I've handed the paper-writing baton to my advisor for a bit.

## 1. Advances in two-photon imaging

An ordinary microscope can only cover a single layer of cells or a thin tissue slice. Two-photon microscopy gets past that limit — using a long-wavelength (infrared) laser, firing it at a precisely timed wavelength and interval, and reconstructing the light reflected back from the sample — letting you watch what's happening much deeper (up to 1mm) in real tissue directly.

At that depth, you can watch changes in a mouse's cortex in real time, as active neurons light up through a calcium-sensitive reporter. With a bit more surgery to look a little deeper, you can watch the hippocampus — which handles memory and sits near the bottom of the human brain but toward the top in mice — in real time too.

But because a two-photon setup is complex, the mouse traditionally has to be head-fixed under the scope, watching a VR environment or walking a track it feels with its paws while immobilized.

For the past few years, miniaturized head-mounted two-photon systems have started appearing, and at this conference they seemed more established than I expected. Mice wearing them can now roam freely while their cortical activity is recorded (lower resolution than the fixed setup, but plenty usable). It felt like real progress toward getting past the constraint of immobility.

## 2. Nanotubes and inter-neuronal signaling

The most fun talk from the postdocs, hands down, was Dr. Minhyuk Jang's (Johns Hopkins) on material and signal transfer between neurons via nanotubes. Filopodia, long thought to be nothing more than a synapse precursor, turn out to be a key building block in the early formation of nanotubes between neurons, and once formed, these nanotubes let calcium and even organelles move quickly to another neuron without going through the synapse at all.

As a neurodegeneration researcher, what interested me most was the Abeta experiment: under Abeta-induced stress, a neuron shares Abeta with other neurons through these nanotubes, and cells that are poor at forming nanotubes end up receiving more than they give — accumulating more relative to neighboring cells and aggregating as a result. A fun idea.

## 3. SVZ neural stem cell migration, viewed evolutionarily

The second-best trainee talk was from Dr. Jaeyeon Kim at UCSF. During development, most neurons stay close to where they were born, but some migrate over remarkably long distances from one region to another. In mice, the subventricular zone (SVZ) — a hotspot of neural stem cells — is where this kind of migration originates, and she looked at how it varies across species, framed evolutionarily.

In mice, 80% of the cells generated in the SVZ and sent elsewhere head toward the olfactory bulb, while mammals with more complex brains show longer, more elaborate migration patterns. In humans specifically, this SVZ cell migration continues until about six months after birth, and beyond the SVZ-to-motor-cortex migration common to all mammals, a second population of neural stem cells heading toward the temporal lobe — which handles language — appears to be unique to humans. Interesting from an evolutionary standpoint, and it got me thinking about whether disruptions to this migration might underlie various developmental disorders.

Both speakers did their PhDs at DGIST, apparently — DGIST's neuroscience department is quietly becoming a real contender.

## 5. A glimpse into bioengineering

Most of the faculty talks were from established names in the field, familiar enough that it mostly felt like "oh, they've added a few more findings." The bioengineering talks, though, which I don't get to see often, were genuinely fresh and fun.

Delivering a gene, or an si/shRNA to suppress one, into the brain needs a carrier. At the bench level, virus-derived vectors are the workhorse. Modify an adeno-associated virus — one that only replicates with help from an adenovirus — and you get a carrier that doesn't replicate once inside but can still deliver and express the gene you want. But these viruses cross the blood-brain barrier poorly and tend to accumulate in the liver, causing hepatotoxicity as a side effect. Engineered variants like AAV-PHP.eB maximize BBB crossing, and later ones like AAV.CAP-B10 both cross the BBB and largely spare the liver.

The other technique that surprised me was expansion microscopy. Fix the tissue, embed it in a kind of hydrogel, and physically expand the whole thing nearly 20-fold in size — like scaling up an image in Photoshop or Illustrator. The hydrogel holds the molecules in place as it swells, so their relative positions stay proportionally intact. This makes previously invisible fine structures large enough to see without needing super-resolution imaging, and in cases where proteins are packed too densely for antibody staining to work well — synaptic proteins, or neurodegeneration-related aggregates — the expansion apparently makes staining noticeably easier too.

## 6. Afterthoughts

Small enough a conference that I ended up talking with a lot of the attendees. Most conversations circled around Parkinson's, my bread and butter, and autism, which I've grown more interested in lately — everyone had real concerns about the current state of Parkinson's models, and people said autism was even harder to get a handle on, since at least Parkinson's has a model to work from. With a neurodegenerative disease you at least have one shared aggregating protein to build a story around; several engineering researchers pointed out that autism research struggles far more to find any shared change — structural or molecular — across cases.

From how to build slides, to introducing yourself and steering a conversation, to asking the right questions and updating on the information you need — this was a conference where I learned a lot, small things included.

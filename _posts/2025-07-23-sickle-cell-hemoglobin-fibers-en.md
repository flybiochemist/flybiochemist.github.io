Hemoglobin is made of two subunit types, alpha and beta. The mutation behind sickle cell disease changes a single amino acid in the beta subunit's sequence — a negatively charged glutamic acid becomes a neutral, hydrophobic valine, altering how the protein's structure can interact. Oxygen-bound, there's no problem. Deoxygenated, though, the mutant beta subunits are now free to bind other deoxygenated mutant beta subunits. One mutant hemoglobin's beta subunit binds another's, and another, until tens or hundreds of thousands of hemoglobin molecules link into a fiber.

These linked hemoglobin fibers cause two problems. First, hemoglobin locked into such a long fiber can no longer bind oxygen, which reduces how much oxygen a single red blood cell can actually carry (equivalently, how much of its hemoglobin remains active).

Second, these hemoglobin polymer fibers grow long, slightly curved, and branching, building a scaffold inside the red blood cell that shouldn't exist (made entirely of hemoglobin fiber). Like a stick pushed into a balloon — when the air lets out, the stick bends and the balloon deflates into a crescent shape — the red cell reshaped by this beta-subunit scaffold naturally has far less flexibility than a normal, round red cell with no internal scaffold, and struggles to squeeze through narrow capillaries. A cell already bad at carrying oxygen then goes and blocks the vessel too, leaving tissue chronically starved of oxygen.

## Four treatments

There are currently four treatment approaches.

1. Bone marrow transplant, replacing the stem cells entirely
2. Suppressing beta-globin expression during red cell differentiation
3. Binding the oxygen-bound mutant beta subunit to block it from releasing its oxygen
4. Gene-edited correction of hematopoietic stem cells

The first, bone marrow transplant, runs into the difficulty of finding a well-matched donor and the immune complications that follow.

The second uses hydroxyurea to shift hemoglobin gene expression during red cell differentiation toward the gamma form — the version normally expressed heavily in the fetus — alongside the usual alpha and beta, producing hemoglobin made of two alpha and two gamma subunits instead of two alpha and two beta. Red cells rich in this fetal-type hemoglobin carry relatively little beta globin, so the resulting fibers are shorter (fewer beta subunits means less chance of them meeting each other), which improves oxygen-carrying capacity and reduces the number of misshapen red cells.

The third is a compound drug called Voxelotor, which binds the oxygen-bound mutant beta subunit and blocks it from releasing its oxygen. Since oxygen-bound beta subunits don't bind each other to form fibers, this works by preventing fiber formation in the first place.

The fourth removes the patient's own hematopoietic stem cells, corrects just the mutant gene outside the body, wipes out the existing stem cell population inside the patient with chemotherapy, and reinfuses the corrected cells. Of all the current methods, this is the one that most directly fixes the underlying cause. The cost pressure is still significant, but it's likely the most effective approach we have.

For single-gene diseases, especially ones where the relevant organ (the liver, say, or hematopoietic stem cells) is comparatively easy to swap cells in or to deliver gene correction to, treatments like this should keep getting easier to develop.

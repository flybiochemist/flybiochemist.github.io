Parkinson's is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, though 'second' does not quite convey how it feels. Alzheimer's, the most common, has an overwhelming prevalence of 5% at 65 and 33% at 85, while Parkinson's runs under 0.5% at 65 and 2% at 85. Worldwide, though, that is around nine million patients, concentrated (life expectancy being what it is) in middle-income countries and above. Judged by quality of life for patients and their families and by rising medical costs, it is the disease after Alzheimer's for which we most need to find a treatment quickly.

Five to twenty years before Parkinson's characteristic symptoms appear — tremor, slowed movement, rigidity, postural instability — a few prodromal signs show up. Constipation and reduced sense of smell among them, and I single out those two because they connect to the question in the title.

## Pathology that begins in the olfactory bulb and the vagus nerve

In the early 2000s the German anatomist Heiko Braak analysed brains across the full range from early to late Parkinson's, and found that Lewy bodies, the major marker of the disease, appear first in two places: the olfactory bulb and the vagus nerve.

It is easiest to think of the vagus nerve as connecting the midbrain to the nerves of the body. As Parkinson's symptoms progress, Lewy bodies appear to spread from these two sites into the rest of the brain.

At stages 3–4, when they spread to the substantia nigra — the dense dopaminergic region of the midbrain — dopaminergic cell death is accompanied by declining motor ability. As the pathology later extends into cortical regions, visual hallucinations and psychosis appear. The gut, where constipation shows up, is where the vagus nerve connects; and the olfactory bulb somehow seems connected to the loss of function described earlier. Just a remarkable coincidence?

## Could cutting the vagus nerve prevent Parkinson's?

Putting all that together: if protein clumps like Lewy bodies, formed in the constipated gut, travel up the vagus nerve as a road to reach the dopaminergic cells of the midbrain and cause trouble there, then could blocking the road — cutting the vagus nerve where it comes up from the gut — lower the incidence of Parkinson's?

In 2017, a Swedish group published population data showing that people who had undergone vagotomy had significantly reduced Parkinson's incidence compared with those who had not. In 2020, Han Seok Ko's lab at Johns Hopkins showed in mice that vagotomy could block the movement of protein aggregates resembling Lewy bodies.

## Open questions

We still do not know why aggregation begins first in these two places, the nose and the gut. But plenty of people are interested in the hypothesis that these two sites matter, and that blocking the spread of Lewy bodies will matter for the disease.

Current proposals for blocking that progression include antibodies against alpha-synuclein, the main constituent protein of Lewy bodies; inhibitors of its aggregation; preventing neuronal death and easing inflammation, on the premise that aggregate spread happens as neurons die; and activating the cell's own machinery for clearing aggregated protein. One slightly odd fact: smokers have the same or, in some studies, lower odds of developing Parkinson's than non-smokers. Smoking has a great many negative effects, of course, but something in the smoke may yet be used as a future Parkinson's inhibitor. There is a long way to go.

Still, if you take the nose-and-gut hypothesis seriously, then beyond tobacco, avoiding contact between the nose and gut and harmful substances — pesticides in particular, which damage mitochondria — as far as possible may be where prevention starts for a disease that will arrive in roughly five people in a thousand.

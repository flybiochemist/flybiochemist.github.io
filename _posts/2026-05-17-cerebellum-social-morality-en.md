One of the most surprising things I ever heard was from Professor Han Hye-min, on the relationship between the cerebellum and morality. To most brain scientists the cerebellum is the seat of motor control, studied almost entirely through the lens of movement disorders — but in humans, apparently, it's involved in morality too.

I thought that was such a fun fact that I brought it up as small talk with my own advisor. They responded as if it were obvious — there had long been plenty of papers on the cerebellum's importance to social behavior in mice (with a look that said "you didn't know this?"). Turns out it's a field with a fairly steady body of work behind it.

## Old clues: an autopsy report from 1988

The paper my advisor pointed me to was from 1988(!): a postmortem study of autistic patients showing reduced volume in specific cerebellar folia (lobules VI and VII). Earlier reports had also linked cerebellar injury to reduced sociality and cognitive decline.

To keep this from sprawling, let me stick to autism. There had already been plenty of case reports establishing that "the cerebellum is affected in autism." But since the cerebellum handles movement, and the temporal lobe, frontal lobe, and amygdala were already overflowing fields of study on their own, the cerebellum was probably assumed to be a bystander in this area.

Then, as more work kept coming out showing the cerebellum's importance in addiction and emotional processing, it seems the old findings got dug back up and research into the cerebellum and sociality picked back up. (I keep hedging because I genuinely don't know the history well.) The study that stands out most to me is a 2012 Nature paper.

## Cerebellar Purkinje cells and sociality

Researchers made a mouse carrying a mutation in TSC1, an autism-associated gene. The mouse shows reduced sociality, and the most notable change is a drop in the number and function of cerebellar Purkinje cells. A TSC1 inhibitor (rapamycin) was found to improve the reduced sociality.

As a follow-up, the same group published in Nature Neuroscience in 2017, proposing that the cerebellar Crus I circuit is what links to this sociality. In both patients and Tsc1-mutant mice, this circuit shows sparser connectivity; suppressing that same connectivity in normal mice produced the social deficit. Conversely, forcibly activating the circuit in Tsc1 mice improved their sociality.

Obviously two papers alone can't tell you whether this is specific to the TSC1 mutation or a broader, generalizable phenomenon. Fortunately, around the same time, a wave of studies began mapping out previously unknown connections between the cerebellum and various cortical circuits, and the cerebellum slowly stopped looking like the brain's indifferent motor-only region.

## The cerebellum-VTA connection and sociality

One region found to link the cerebellum to various parts of the cortex is the VTA. Already central to reward and sociality on its own, a 2020 Science paper found it connects to the DCN, the cerebellum's output structure — and cutting that connection produced reduced sociality.

## Summary

I'm writing this pretty much stream-of-consciousness and it's wandering, but to sum up:

1. The cerebellum does not only handle movement.
2. The cerebellum plays a real role in sociality too.

That much might make for a decent bit of small talk sometime.

## Bonus

If you work on Parkinson's: the VTA sits right below the SNpc, the region where dopaminergic neuron death is measured, and stains for TH the same way. If your VTA looks different, keep in mind it might be confounding any sociality or reward-related experiment. (This is one reason I don't love MPTP.)

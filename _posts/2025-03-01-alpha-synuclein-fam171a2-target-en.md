To talk shop for once: a rather good-looking drug target went up in Science this week. Explaining why it looks good means first saying a little about alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's.

In the brains of Parkinson's patients you often find protein-plus-lipid aggregates called Lewy bodies, and what is packed densely inside them is phosphorylated alpha-synuclein.

The curious part is that as Parkinson's progresses, Lewy bodies appear to spread from particular regions — the olfactory bulb and the vagus nerve — to others. When this is mimicked by injecting an appropriately sized clump of alpha-synuclein into an animal brain, pathology appears first at the injection site, and the aggregated alpha-synuclein then goes on to make more aggregated alpha-synuclein, much like the prion in prion disease (famous in Korea as mad cow disease), which then spreads to other regions, producing this pathology and these symptoms.

## Could you block the door the aggregate enters through?

That lets you hypothesise that removing the alpha-synuclein aggregates moving from one region to another with an antibody might treat Parkinson's. Several alpha-synuclein antibodies are being made on this basis. But antibodies are expensive to produce and come with the inconvenience of injection into cerebrospinal fluid.

So some people thought: why not instead block the aggregate from entering the next neuron? The argument was that there is presumably a receptor that recognises the aggregate at the cell surface and takes it in. Several genes have already been named as candidate receptors (LRP1, TLR2/4, LAG3) and drug development is under way. But some of the existing candidates have important functions in non-neuronal cells, immune cells especially, and others are either not expressed in neurons or expressed so highly that no reasonable dose of drug can inhibit them. Hence the demand for a suitable target.

## A new candidate: FAM171A2

According to this Science paper, the authors used UK Biobank, FinnGen and a Chinese patient database to pinpoint FAM171A2, a gene with increased mutation in Parkinson's patients.

The criteria were presumably that it 1) be highly expressed in dopaminergic cells, and 2) act as a receptor such that the more of the protein there is, the less alpha-synuclein circulates in cerebrospinal fluid, since it would have been taken into cells. Mice overexpressing this protein developed Parkinson's symptoms quickly when given alpha-synuclein aggregates, while mice with the gene completely removed barely showed symptoms at all.

Interestingly, as a receptor the protein interacted specifically with the aggregated form of alpha-synuclein rather than the normal one. The team then showed the meeting of the two molecules by NMR, selected a compound based on structural prediction, and found that it effectively blocked alpha-synuclein aggregates from entering cells.

The paper is also a good illustration of the rise of Chinese science. It comes from three corresponding authors out of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai and Fudan University, and tracking their publications shows five to ten papers a year, spread evenly across Cell and other high-level journals. Their labs are not even especially large. The efficiency seems high.

If Parkinson's progression could be slowed by three small-molecule tablets a day that cross the blood-brain barrier, rather than an expensive cerebrospinal injection you can only get at a hospital, that is a decent-looking prospect. It could of course fall over along the way, but I hope work in this direction continues and keeps contributing to Parkinson's treatment.

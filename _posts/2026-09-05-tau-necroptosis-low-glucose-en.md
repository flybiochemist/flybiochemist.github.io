These days we have enough good biomarkers that this gets less attention, but 20-odd years ago, the way to measure trouble in disease-relevant brain regions — in Alzheimer's or other neurodegenerative diseases — was either to measure physical volume (MRI) or to inject glucose tagged with a weak radioactive isotope and track its metabolism (FDG-PET). In Alzheimer's, that showed reduced glucose uptake in regions like the frontal cortex and hippocampus.

A drop in glucose was assumed to bring on dysfunction in neurons and glia, plus stress and byproducts from the mitochondrial shift needed to cover for it (switching from glucose to ketones or lactate) — and plenty of papers have chased that angle.

Today's paper goes a step further: it argues that tau, Alzheimer's headline protein, directly kills cells when glucose runs low.

## Low glucose, tau, and necroptosis

Tau is the main component of the tangles that are always found aggregated inside neurons in Alzheimer's patients. Overexpressing a single phosphorylation mutant is enough to reproduce the memory decline seen in dementia, and a phosphorylated form at a specific site is one of the key markers used in blood-based dementia diagnostics. Plenty of hypotheses have circulated about how this phosphorylated, aggregated tau actually kills neurons.

The hook of this paper — arguably one of the boldest hypotheses in the field lately — is that a signaling pathway activated under low glucose (A20 loss and p-P38) uses phosphorylated tau as a stable scaffold that amplifies binding to RIPK1, the switch that triggers necroptosis (a cell-death pathway distinct from apoptosis, closer to plain necrosis). That triggers necroptosis, the neuron dies, tau spills out of the cell, neighboring cells take it up, and those cells die too — a vicious cycle complete.

The authors show that boosting A20 back up rescues the phenotype to some degree, but bumping up a gene's expression or protein levels is a lot harder than knocking it down, and the necroptosis data tied to this specific mechanism is on the thinner side.

Still, it was fun to watch two hallmark Alzheimer's phenomena — low glucose uptake and phospho-tau accumulation — get wired together into a single cell-death story in one paper.

## A research lineage running from the US to the next generation back home

This paper also comes out of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai. A lot of the foundational work on cell death pathways beyond plain apoptosis — including necroptosis, the subject here — was done by Chinese American scientists in the US, and seeing their students now running this kind of integrative research back in China makes me a little envious of that chain: the first generation that built the field in the US, now handing it to a second generation at home.

Tangent aside, as someone in the field, I enjoyed this one.

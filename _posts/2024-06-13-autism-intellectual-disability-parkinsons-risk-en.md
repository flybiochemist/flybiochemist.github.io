A fairly striking talk was given last month at the annual neurodiversity meeting in Melbourne, Australia. A tracking study of roughly 200,000 people aged 40 and over with autism, intellectual disability, or both found that this group developed Parkinson's at more than three times the rate of those without. Not yet even a published paper, this study still succeeded in drawing the field's attention (and some of its side-eye) enough to be covered as news in Nature.

Scattered related work from the 1940s through the mid-2010s stayed in the realm of hypothesis, with fewer than a hundred patients across all of it combined. But now we have reached the point of needing to answer why. One argument attributes it to the medications used in treatment.

## A personal hypothesis

My own suspicion is that the genes causing autism and intellectual disability are themselves involved. Many autism-related genes encode proteins that handle the release of neurotransmitter at the synapse and its subsequent recycling — some of these are already known to cause Parkinson's, and even the ones that are not do similar work to the proteins that do. If a mutation impairs one of these proteins, it could overload the similarly functioning Parkinson's-causing proteins, and that could speed up how quickly Parkinson's arrives.

Granted, for someone with autism this is a rise from 1–2% to 6% — the odds of staying healthy only drop from 98–99% to 94%. But as more and more children diagnosed with autism grow up managing it well through various therapies, it may be worth paying closer attention to prodromal signs once those children reach their forties and fifties.

I have mentioned before the use of 40Hz light or sound to lower Alzheimer's culprit proteins, and it remains quite controversial. Various mouse experiments disagree over whether it works or fails to reproduce, and Korean researchers recently published a paper pinning down detailed conditions and leaning toward reproducibility.

## A different result in monkeys

Since almost all the related work so far has been in mice, a Chinese team ran the experiment in a monkey model instead. Rhesus macaques — the animal most people picture when they think "monkey" — were given 40Hz sound for one hour a day over one week, and the team measured amyloid-beta and tau pathology, the presumed hallmark and cause of Alzheimer's, in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and postmortem brain. Unlike in mice, CSF Abeta concentration more than doubled, and that persisted six weeks later.

They then verified amyloid-beta and tau pathology by tissue staining, and here the group given 40Hz sound versus the control group came out with one severely affected monkey and one moderately affected monkey — indistinguishable (n=2). What is clear is that nothing showed a difference on the scale of the doubling seen in CSF.

## How to explain ambiguous data

Since rerunning the experiment in monkeys for a revision is essentially impossible, the group explains this ambiguous data as follows:

1. Given no difference in amyloid or tau pathology within the brain, the CSF increase might reflect activation of the glymphatic system (the brain's lymphatic drainage), which has been much discussed lately — though this cannot be proven right now (they say they will, next time, for sure).

2. So (not stated explicitly) people who already believe this method works can point to it as evidence it works, and people who don't can point to the rise in amyloid-beta as evidence it doesn't.

If you are the shrimp caught between two whales in this fight, it might be worth studying this miraculous dodge closely.

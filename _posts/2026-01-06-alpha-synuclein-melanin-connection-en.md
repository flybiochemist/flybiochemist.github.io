Alpha-synuclein is the protein most strongly suspected of causing Parkinson's disease. The standard marker for diagnosing a Parkinson's patient is the Lewy body, in which alpha-synuclein is aggregated, and the most widely accepted hypothesis remains that aggregated protein kills dopaminergic neurons and brings on the disease.

So where is alpha-synuclein outside the brain, and what does it do there? A question I happen to like. The tissue with the highest gene expression outside the brain is blood cells; the protein is found in greatest quantity in kidney, bladder, cartilage, skin, and blood cells. And there is one thing skin and the brain's dopaminergic neurons have in common: melanin, the pigment that produces black colour. The region where the dopaminergic neurons dying in Parkinson's are gathered is even named for it — substantia nigra, so called because the tissue is black with neuromelanin. Is there a connection?

## A clue from melanocytes in skin

Neuromelanin in neurons and the melanins of skin (eumelanin, pheomelanin) differ somewhat in structure and function, but each protects tissue and cells from hostile conditions, ROS and UV respectively. And as it happens, alpha-synuclein is abundant wherever this melanin is. Melanoma, cancer of the skin's melanocytes, occurs at 1.4 to 20 times the rate in Parkinson's patients. Dopamine, meanwhile, is the biochemical precursor of melanin — the raw material. Something seems to be connected.

What is known so far about melanin-producing skin cells and alpha-synuclein:

1. Removing alpha-synuclein causes no particular problem for melanin production.
2. But overexpressing alpha-synuclein under UV exposure reduces melanin production.
3. Cell-level experiments established that raising alpha-synuclein breaks intracellular trafficking and secretion, and signs of this appear in melanocytes too.

## In neurons it works the other way

In neurons, by contrast, overexpressing alpha-synuclein produces more neuromelanin. And neuromelanin, which ought to be protective, is thought to keep harming the cell: given that these cells do not divide, once it is made it stays with the person until they die of old age, never diluted into daughter cells. The grounds:

1. In monkey models where neuromelanin was lowered below some threshold, Parkinson's symptoms eased.
2. There are monkeys in which raising neuromelanin alone produced Parkinson's symptoms.

This would be hard to see in mice, though, since mouse dopaminergic cells have no neuromelanin. And as it happens, dopaminergic cell death is also hard to see in mice. Neuromelanin again?

There is a long way to go, but because Parkinson's research relies mainly on mouse models, the relationship between melanin and alpha-synuclein has probably been understudied. There may yet be attempts to treat Parkinson's by manipulating melanin.

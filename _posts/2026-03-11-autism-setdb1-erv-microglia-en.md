Three things are worth knowing before this paper makes sense.

1. Our bodies carry DNA derived from retroviruses, and under stress this DNA gets transcribed more, or even jumps out of one spot in the genome and lands in another. So when the genes that keep chromatin tightly wound or loosen it lose function, these sequences can start running wild again.

2. One cause of autism is altered signalling at the neuron's terminal. Whether from a gene defect or from an overactive maternal immune response during pregnancy — where antibodies crossing to the fetus trigger a neuroimmune reaction — changes at the synapse can push neuron-to-neuron signalling too high or too low, and either can cause autism.

3. Signalling within a neuron travels electrically via voltage; signalling between neurons crosses through a connection called the synapse. A signalling molecule released from one neuron's postsynaptic terminal has to reach the presynaptic side of the next neuron for signalling to continue down the line, relay-race style. Microglia, which handle immunity around neurons, prune synapses to keep them properly formed — but in disease, excessive pruning becomes the problem.

## No SETDB1, no synapse

The gene this paper focuses on, SETDB1, has been linked to autism since the early 2000s. A mutation in it alone is enough to cause autism, and patients often show reduced expression of it even without a mutation. Mice with a neuron-specific SETDB1 mutation show reduced social behaviour, and both the sending and receiving ends of their synapses are broadly diminished.

But the neurons themselves did not die. Something was affecting only the synapse. Investigating further, the authors found microglia had over-pruned synaptic branches. But the gene had been removed only from neurons. Something, then, must be made in the neuron, travel to the synapse, and signal "prune me."

Collecting and examining gene expression from just the SETDB1-deficient neurons in the SETDB1 conditional knockout mouse, the authors found C4b, a complement protein, among the genes with elevated expression. Complement proteins are mainly made in infection and inflammation, and naturally activate microglia, which handle nearly all immune function in the brain. The hypothesis — SETDB1 loss, C4b rise, microglial activation, excessive synaptic pruning, autism symptoms — held up reasonably well: removing microglia, or removing C4b, prevented the synaptic loss even without SETDB1.

## A neuron startled by the virus inside itself

But SETDB1 works at the histone level, loosening or tightening chromatin to broadly activate or repress genes — it does not directly regulate the region where C4b sits. So it must raise "something" indirectly, and that something is what then drives C4b production.

So the hunt for "something" continued. ChIP-seq and related analyses showed that what SETDB1 directly regulated was endogenous retrovirus-derived DNA (ERV). This viral-derived DNA, which entered the human chromosome long ago and failed to leave, becoming part of the human genome, goes through the same cycle a virus does when expressed — it is made into RNA, reverse-transcribed back into DNA, and reinserted. The problem is that this RNA-DNA hybrid intermediate is indistinguishable from what an actual virus produces, and when the cell detects it, it defends itself by making complement proteins like C4b. The neuron, startled by the ERV inside itself, made C4b and got its synapses pruned for it.

## An AIDS drug improves the symptoms

Treatment with a reverse transcriptase inhibitor, extensively developed as an AIDS drug, stopped C4b production and improved symptoms. The same inhibitors also worked well in mice with environmentally driven autism from maternal immune activation (MIA). It suggests we need to look closely at this ERV-C4b-microglia synaptic control mechanism specifically in autism driven by SETDB1 dysfunction or by environmental factors like MIA.

Of course, "the common cold" is a catch-all name for infection symptoms caused by over 200 different viruses, and autism likewise spans many types, so a single reverse transcriptase inhibitor will not cure everything. But work like this may let us subdivide autism further and propose more targeted treatments.

A good deal of work has recently gone into the relationship between Parkinson's and immunity outside the brain, particularly in the gut.

1. Inflammation outside the brain raises phosphorylation of alpha-synuclein in peripheral neurons.
2. But those peripheral neurons show neither the aggregated alpha-synuclein seen in Parkinson's patients' brains nor neuronal death.
3. Even so, having an inflammatory disease raises the chance of developing Parkinson's.
4. So how do we connect peripheral nerve (mostly gut), inflammation and synuclein aggregation to Parkinson's?

The authors of today's paper modelled the interaction between enteric neurons and gut immune cells — particularly ME-MACs, muscularis macrophages — under the inflammatory conditions of Parkinson's, using two approaches: a mouse model overexpressing alpha-synuclein in neurons (including enteric neurons), and injection of alpha-synuclein aggregates into the gut.

## Macrophages with indigestion from eating aggregates

Two things are newly established here on top of the recent discussion.

First, the phosphorylation that precedes alpha-synuclein aggregation happens in enteric neurons stressed by inflammation — but the surrounding macrophages take that up and, while degrading it, eat so much that they choke, and in that state they instead produce a more robust synuclein aggregate. And this aggregate is highly infectious: mere contact with another synuclein protein converts it into the same twisted aggregate. This proposes that the origin of peripherally derived alpha-synuclein aggregates lies not in neurons alone but in an interaction with macrophages in the gut, specifically in the muscularis layer.

Second, macrophages can present antigen. That activates T cells. And among those T cells, killer T cells may well run to the brain, which by old age is full of alpha-synuclein aggregates, and kill neurons.

## Remove the macrophages and progression slows

So: these ME-MACs both make the aggregate and mount such an excessive immune response to it that harm outweighs protection. If you removed them entirely, might progression slow? Blocking the production of these macrophages genetically, or inhibiting them with a compound, turned out to protect a good number of the midbrain dopaminergic neurons — the ones whose death produces the Parkinson's motor symptoms we know. A decent approach.

The work was done at UCL in England, and one of the corresponding authors is Professor Soyon Hong, well known for studies of microglia and neurodegeneration.

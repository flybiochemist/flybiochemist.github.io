Michael J. Fox, known to my generation as the actor from Back to the Future, was diagnosed with Parkinson's at 30, at the height of his fame just after finishing the trilogy. He went on to found the Michael J. Fox Foundation (MJFF) and has been active ever since — so much so that Gen Z Parkinson's researchers know him only as a foundation figure.

Michael once travelled to Bhutan, and in a place that starts at the altitude of Baekdusan he felt his Parkinson's symptoms ease. Patients had reported feeling better at high altitude before, too. So what makes the difference? Two theories have circulated: low pressure and low oxygen. Low pressure is hard to do in a lab, so most of the work has gone into low oxygen, dropping the 21% oxygen concentration to 17% (humans) or 11% (mice).

## Mice in low oxygen hold up better

Today's paper studies the improvement of Parkinson's symptoms under low oxygen in a mouse model. To mimic the hypoxia experienced at 4,500–4,999 m, mice were housed for 12 weeks in a chamber with oxygen lowered to 11%. They showed alpha-synuclein aggregation — the protein aggregation thought to cause Parkinson's — comparable to mice housed at normal oxygen over the same period, and yet less dopaminergic neuron damage, with motor ability also preserved.

How was that possible? The researchers looked for the various changes oxygen produces. At 11% oxygen, the gene expression changes seen in Parkinson's were suppressed. And the intracellular peroxidation driven by reactive oxygen species escaping unprocessed from the mitochondria damaged in Parkinson's — in particular the failure of oxidised lipids to function, which has been drawing attention in Parkinson's lately — was substantially reduced.

How long does the protection from low oxygen last? A mouse lives 24 months; Parkinson's mice were kept in low oxygen for 10 months after symptom onset. Remarkably, symptoms did not worsen but held steady. Putting mice with Parkinson's already in progress into low oxygen for six weeks also produced recovery. It cannot treat the underlying cause, but low oxygen can at least help ease symptoms.

From a research standpoint, this makes me think we should be considering the relationship between intracellular reactive oxygen species, oxidised lipids and symptoms. The known Parkinson's genes include mitochondrial ones (Parkin, Pink1) and genes involved in regulating glycolipids in the lysosome (GBA, LRRK2, ATP13A2). And oxygen is a crucial element in metal deposition. Coming away from someone else's work with something to think about is an important ingredient in the next good study.

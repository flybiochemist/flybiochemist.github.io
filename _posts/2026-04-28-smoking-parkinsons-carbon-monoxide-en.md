One of the puzzles in Parkinson's research is why smoking lowers the odds of developing the disease.

One of the main indicators of Parkinson's is loss of the sense of smell. The earliest disease-related Lewy body pathology appears in two places, one of them the olfactory mucosa and the olfactory bulb it connects to. That conditions likely to damage olfactory tissue — exposure to pesticides and herbicides, pollution, particulates — raise the risk of Parkinson's is common sense. And yet in Parkinson's, smoking is protective.

Why?

## Carbon monoxide, not nicotine

Setting aside the bomb of particulate matter a cigarette produces, attention has fallen on roughly two things: nicotine, and the carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion. Of these, the easiest experiment — nicotine, via patches — showed no real improvement. So recent work has concentrated on carbon monoxide. And that story connects straight to the hypoxia that carbon monoxide induces.

Pulling together individual reports that Parkinson's symptoms oddly improve at high altitude, researchers reproduced the low-oxygen conditions characteristic of altitude by dropping ambient oxygen from 21% to 11–17%. The account runs through protection from activation of Hif-1alpha signalling and other hypoxia-induced genes, plus prevention of the excess reactive oxygen species and lipid peroxidation seen in dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra in alpha-synuclein aggregate injection models. Less oxygen, less peroxidation. Very intuitive.

Carbon monoxide also does several things in its own right. In its presence, the activity of glia — astrocytes in particular, the neurons' companions — increases, and they provide protection to the surrounding neurons.

## Protection from a low dose of carbon monoxide

Two years ago a paper applied this to Parkinson's by generating carbon monoxide via an oral route. Having just 4.5–7.8% of haemoglobin temporarily bound to carbon monoxide and unable to carry oxygen produced a fairly durable protective effect in a mouse model. As in the earlier model, various genes whose expression rises under low oxygen were elevated.

Whether it holds in humans is unknown, of course. But rather than smoking, which has nothing to recommend it, a carbon monoxide pill may end up being worth considering.

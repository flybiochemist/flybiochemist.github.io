Between 1979 and 1982, seven men in their twenties in California developed Parkinson's symptoms at an extraordinarily young age. Epidemiological investigation found they had all used heroin manufactured in the same place, and analysis of that heroin showed that another compound, MPTP, had been synthesised during the production of the heroin itself (MPPP). Administration of that compound caused irreversible, permanent damage to dopaminergic neurons and produced Parkinson's disease.

MPTP is metabolised into MPP, and that compound is taken into dopamine-releasing neurons by the dopamine transporter DAT. Then it kills the cell. Because DAT is far more abundant on dopaminergic cells, the same dose selectively kills only those cells. Hence the Parkinson's.

Taking the hint from this episode, mice injected with MPTP became the early Parkinson's model. A tragic event, but one that contributed enormously to the beginning of Parkinson's research.

## Still used, but with real limits

As a Parkinson's model it has plenty of limitations. It does selectively kill dopaminergic neurons, but it skips the whole sequence that occurs in Parkinson's — cells ageing, internal function declining, interactions with neighbouring cells breaking down, dopamine release gradually falling, behavioural abnormalities appearing — and goes straight to the endpoint of dead dopaminergic neurons. You cannot study the intermediate steps. And if you dose it lightly, the cells do not die at all.

Because the dose is high enough to kill cells, it is hard to see improvement from anything you apply; and when something does help, it tends to be a drug that simply blocks sudden cell death, which often does nothing for the slow, continuous dopaminergic loss of actual Parkinson's. Since it is injected, the phenotype varies from batch to batch depending on who is doing the injecting.

Even so it remains one of the three recognised classes of Parkinson's mouse model, and is still favoured by anyone who wants to see a Parkinson's effect quickly.

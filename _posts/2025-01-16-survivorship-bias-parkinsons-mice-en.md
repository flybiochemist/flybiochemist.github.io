A project shared across about six labs in our building studies a rare Parkinson's patient case. Genomic analysis of the patient found mutations in four known Parkinson's-associated genes. But all four were mutations barely seen in Parkinson's patients generally — presumably weak drivers of disease on their own — so the working hypothesis, and the experiment built on it, was that four individually weak mutations might add up, or synergise, when present together.

Among them, though, a mouse carrying a mutation in just one of the four genes showed severe tremor, and mice with two of the four mutated died suddenly at an early age; on examining their brains, dramatic changes turned up. The mutation apparently causes brain pathology so severe that some other disease arrives before Parkinson's ever would, which is presumably why pure Parkinson's cases carrying it are rare. Part of the patient's Parkinson's symptoms may in fact come from this gene's dysfunction, unrelated to dopaminergic neurons, and a drug already used for other diseases linked to different mutations in this gene might work here.

Sometimes the most common mutation is the moderately mild one — a severe mutation can kill the organism outright. It was only after seeing the result that I recognised this as survivorship bias. There is still a great deal in biology that even AI cannot predict (this gene's predicted impact on Parkinson's ranked third out of the four genes) and that you can only learn by building the model yourself.

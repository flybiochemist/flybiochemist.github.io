Two researchers took each other on publicly, in the form of open letters, on Alzforum, which every dementia researcher reads. On one side, Scott A. Small of Columbia; on the other, Dennis J. Selkoe of Harvard Medical School and Brigham and Women's, one of the old hands of the field.

The battle started over an op-ed by Selkoe urging that the next generation of dementia drugs be fast-tracked for approval under the same standard used for the lukewarmly effective beta-amyloid antibody approved in 2023.

Alzheimer's shows amyloid-beta aggregated into plaques outside cells and tau protein aggregated into tangles inside them, and the widely accepted hypothesis in the field holds that amyloid-beta and tau are the cause of the disease. Antibody therapies have accordingly been developed to clear amyloid-beta, and some achieved thorough plaque clearance and a modest delay in dementia symptoms.

## Where the controversy started

These drugs got caught in controversy over a fairly fundamental question: is it right to approve something that merely delays dementia symptoms rather than treating them? Amid the uproar — a review-committee member even resigned in protest — aducanumab and lecanemab ultimately won US approval anyway. But given how modest their effect is, they remain unapproved in Europe, and US insurers are often reluctant to cover them.

Against that backdrop, Selkoe wrote in Alzheimer's & Dementia, the Alzheimer's Association's own journal, arguing that there are 13 lines of scientific evidence that amyloid-beta and tau cause Alzheimer's, that the basis for the last antibody approval was clearance of amyloid plaque from the brain, and that future approvals should lean hard on that same point — plaque-and-tangle reduction plus patient safety. The Alzforum piece covering it quickly filled with comments for and against. And open letters taking direct aim at each other followed.

## A civil war more than 40 years running

The trigger was the definition used to justify fast-track approval: that amyloid-beta (Abeta) plaque outside neurons is the cause of the disease.

This field has been locked in fierce civil war for over 40 years over what actually causes Alzheimer's. To borrow a comparison from Joseon-dynasty factional politics class:

1. The Abeta camp and the tau camp are roughly the Westerners and the Easterners.
2. Within the Abeta camp, arguments over form — oligomer or fibril, and if oligomer, how many subunits — split things further into Noron and Soron.
3. Then there's the Byeokpa, who hold that the visible extracellular plaque is harmless and only intracellular forms are dangerous, versus the Sipa, who say both are toxic.
4. Amid all this, a Tangpyeong faction insists both Abeta and tau matter.
5. And then the outright heretics, who argue the real cause is a cold virus — an absolute madhouse of a field.

(Which is why getting review-assigned to someone in a rival camp rarely ends well for you.)

Someone objecting to a theory held by only "some" of the field being treated as settled fact — that's what set this off.

## Parallel lines, and what's left

Looking only at the outcome, the two of course ended up on largely parallel tracks apart from a few shared points. But for about a month, each laid out the strongest version of their own camp's logic, and anyone watching probably came away with their own sense of who had the better argument. Personally, having been away from the Abeta side of things for a while, this debate was a good way to catch up on where that camp currently stands. (I had no idea SORL1 had become this prominent a gene.)

For funding, for the commercial stakes in future drugs, and simply to hold the field's centre of gravity, this argument will keep going. Still — two scientists opening with a public letter and settling the score with data has a certain romance to it, the kind you'd get from an anime built around duels. (Though from the receiving scientist's perspective it probably feels more like an obsessive, grinding ordeal — easy for me to say, since it's not my fight.)

A gene is a single blueprint, but the proteins made from it come in slightly different versions — isoforms — depending on the tissue and the cell. Tau, for example, can be assembled in six combinations: how many of the two N-terminal blocks (exons) are present (0N/1N/2N), and whether the middle R region has three repeats or four (3R/4R). Proteins of varying length arising from the same gene are one source of cell- and organ-specific variation.

So how does one blueprint produce different products? Through splicing. mRNA contains introns, stretches that are not translated into protein, and various regulatory proteins bind there and determine which introns and which exons get cut out. Interfere with that binding and the splicing does not happen.

## A new opening for drug development

Proteins made differently through splicing offer several possibilities to people developing therapeutics.

The most obvious approach: if the region carrying a mutation can be removed by splicing, remove it. Either suppress the form containing the mutated exon, or promote a different splice so that the relative amount of normal protein goes up.

Another approach applies when a mutation has reduced or increased a protein's function — steer the cell toward an isoform with similar function but a longer half-life. For proteins where a shift of 20–30% is enough to cause problems, this is a good option too. (Making an actual drug out of it is considerably harder, of course.)

Hence the active interest in the intronic regions where the splicing machinery binds. And for this, human cells are more useful than mice.

## Why RNA is the right window

The best option, obviously, would be to cut the mutation out with CRISPR-Cas9 or another editor. But off-target cutting elsewhere in the genome is common, delivery all the way into the nucleus is hard, and editing a genome packed tightly inside chromatin is harder still.

That leaves a window: after nuclear DNA has been copied into RNA and reached the ribosome outside the nucleus, but before translation into protein. Right now that is the sweet spot for gene-level therapy. The approach is to target the mRNA with a single-stranded RNA or DNA carrying a complementary sequence, so that it binds in advance to the region where splicing would occur. Add modifications that resist nucleases and you extend the half-life, so one molecule can regulate the splicing of many mRNAs repeatedly. A small molecule would be better on both cost and access, of course, but that will not be easy.

This is a field I expect to develop.

Parkinson's is found through tremor and loss of control over muscle movement, but the cause is that dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra die, dopamine release is impaired, and motor control by the neurons along the nigrostriatal pathway declines. The fastest way to fix that is either to (1) put dopamine in, or (2) put dopaminergic neurons in from outside to replace the dead ones.

## From fetal tissue grafts to induced pluripotent stem cells

Various attempts have been made since the cause was identified in the 1960s. Since dopamine cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, treating with a precursor that can (levodopa and others) proved effective, and it is still used as a drug today. Elsewhere there were radical experiments in which tissue rich in dopaminergic neurons was taken from the brains of embryos that had sadly not survived, and transplanted into the striatum of a patient's brain.

But the benefit of such surgery varied enormously, and there were side effects that actually widened the swings in Parkinson's symptoms. The conclusion was that this probably arose because cells other than pure dopaminergic ones came along and grew too. So you would want to make only dopaminergic cells — and in the eighties and nineties there was not yet enough technology to solve that.

Then, on top of methods for establishing embryonic stem cells, Shinya Yamanaka's team in 2007 introduced a way to make pluripotent induced stem cells from already-differentiated adult cells by manipulating four genes, the Yamanaka factors. The foundation for making dopamine neurons was laid. From there it became an endless race over who could first make neurons that were both safe and good at differentiating into dopamine neurons, and how to transplant them. The biggest news of this kind in Korea was probably the patient-derived iPSC transplant carried out by Professor Kwang-Soo Kim of Harvard Medical School in 2017.

## From bespoke to off-the-shelf

Patient-matched stem cells showed a clear effect — dramatic early improvement, then a stable state for six years — but being patient-matched, they are hard to mass-produce and bring the price down. So work continues on whether embryonic stem cells, or iPSCs made to match the immunoglobulin types that the largest number of people carry, might allow transplantation into comparatively many people from a common source.

A few days ago two papers ran back to back in Nature: phase 1/2 trials of making and transplanting dopamine neurons, one using embryonic stem cells and the other using iPSCs derived from blood cells. The focus is still on safety, and the papers report that the transplanted cells appear to make dopamine well. There are plenty of mountains left, and since the brain conditions killing dopaminergic neurons have not been improved, other treatments addressing that are also needed. But today too, one more step of scientific progress is contributing to easing one disease.

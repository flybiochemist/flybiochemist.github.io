There is a gene called LRRK2. It is a kinase: it takes a phosphate from ATP and attaches it to a protein, and a protein with a phosphate attached is usually activated. In some LRRK2 mutations, a change in a few amino acids makes it easier for the enzyme to activate other proteins. Carriers of LRRK2 mutations have a higher chance of developing Parkinson's, and in Parkinson's the phosphorylation of this kinase's targets — the Rab GTPases — is increased.

One particular LRRK2 mutation, G2019S, pushes the chance of developing Parkinson's as high as 50%. It is a relatively small share of Parkinson's overall, but among North Africans and Ashkenazi Jews it is common, at 10–30%. It is also the mutation famously carried by Google founder Sergey Brin and his mother.

## The strategy: lower expression with siRNA

So there has long been an approach that tries to suppress LRRK2 expression itself and thereby its function. Editing the gene directly with CRISPR-Cas9 or another editor carries risk, so the most common attempts at gene-level control target RNA, the intermediate step on the way to protein. One such method is siRNA.

Cells have a defence mechanism that cuts up and destroys genetic material of viral origin. Our own RNA is single-stranded, while some viruses carry RNA in a double helix like DNA. When the cell sees that, it goes straight in and shreds the RNA. So if you want to reduce a gene's expression, you can supply an RNA complementary to the transcript, form a double helix, and let the cell's antiviral machinery cut it away. That is siRNA.

But if the siRNA is degraded along with its target, the half-life is too short and you would have to keep dosing. So researchers attached slightly different chemistry to the natural RNA and made modified, degradation-resistant siRNA. Now when the siRNA binds its target, forms a duplex and the target is destroyed, the altered structure means the siRNA itself is less likely to be degraded and more likely to be recycled. It can meet another target RNA, bind, form a duplex and repeat. One injection lasts a good while.

## It engaged the target, but symptoms did not improve

Ionis is a company that is good at making these siRNAs; Biogen is good at carrying a neurodegeneration drug from the beginning through to the end of a clinical trial. The two joined forces and, from 2019, spent six years trying LRRK2-targeting siRNA therapy in Parkinson's patients. In cerebrospinal fluid they could see reduced LRRK2 and reduced phosphorylation of the kinase's Rab targets. It engaged.

But on the measure that matters most in Parkinson's — improvement in motor symptoms — there was not much effect. Even at the highest dose, and even in patients carrying LRRK2 mutations, where the siRNA regulates LRRK2 broadly and the effect should be clearest, there was no large benefit.

We still do not know how well it acted in patients. Whether the siRNA was delivered deep enough into the substantia nigra or striatum, and how well it regulated the gene once there, is unknown. Delivery efficiency, the RNA degradation efficiency of that siRNA, and how far the activity of the remaining protein compensates for the function lost — there is a lot to consider. It is early.

With better delivery methods and vehicles, and better siRNA design, this may yet become a drug.

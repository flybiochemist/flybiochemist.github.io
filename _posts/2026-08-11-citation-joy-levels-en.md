To researchers, being cited is a little less thrilling than getting your own paper out, but it is still a real source of pride — to outsiders, probably a strange thing to feel excited about at all.

Citations have tiers. The lowest tier of joy is showing up as reference 7 of 12 in a review paper's "such-and-such has been reported" list. The genuinely exciting tier is when a paper's discussion section singles your idea out and calls it central. The greatest joy of all is when someone treats a system you built as the standard against which to run their own experiment.

A paper recently came out of a lab at another university that independently tested the Parkinson's mouse model I built, and found that a specific Parkinson's-associated phenotype — one seen in a different Parkinson's mouse carrying an LRRK2 mutation — reproduced well in mine. I worry constantly about how well my creation is doing out in the world on its own, and every time good news like this comes back, I feel absurdly proud as its maker.

Of course this is a pleasure that means nothing to most people's daily lives, one that only lands within our own small field. (And whoever dislikes my model dislikes it a lot.) But this is the flavor that keeps you doing research.

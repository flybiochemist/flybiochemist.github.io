Take skin cells from a patient with a brain-related disease, reprogram them into stem cells, differentiate those into neurons, and build a pseudo-brain out of them via an organoid — this is the research method that has been getting established over the past decade.

Our building recently gained a new faculty member out of the Sergiu Pasca lab, and with protocols and technical advice now flowing from that group, more people around here are taking up brain organoids, so I have been watching with interest.

Organoids obviously still have limits. With no blood vessels and no heart to pump anything, oxygen and nutrients can only be absorbed, and carbon dioxide and waste expelled, by molecular diffusion — so past a certain size, the cells at the core simply die off.

## Watching signal propagation with assembloids

Even so, fusing organoids representing different brain regions into an assembloid puts us at the starting line for building a connected, functioning brain. Fuse cortex-thalamus-spinal-cord along the front-back axis, or dorsal/ventral cortex, and you can watch a signal that starts in one neuron spread outward like a wave.

Whether you're modelling development or an adult neurodegenerative disease should change how you assemble the organoid-to-assembloid system.

Unfortunately the group in our lab making the most active use of this is the autism side, so for now I am getting most of my indirect exposure to how organoids are used through developmental brain disorders.

Beyond the gene differences between human and mouse, the regulatory regions controlling gene expression — promoters, enhancers — differ enormously, and DNA- or mRNA-targeted therapies can vary tens of times in effect from a single base difference. So for miRNA/siRNA-based therapy research, where the major disease and the regulatory region happen to match between mouse and human, someone has usually already done the mouse work — which leaves you with iPSC-derived neurons or organoids as the only option. Transplanting into a mouse brain even helps get past some of the size problem.

## Three trends I'm making sure not to miss

Three things I'm not yet fluent in but am working to at least understand, so I don't fall behind on the latest trends: first, identifying disease-associated genes from population genomic data and the methodology behind it; second, the intersection of AI and experiment, especially AI-based prediction tools; third, organoid-assembloid disease research, particularly neurodevelopmental disorders and some neurodegenerative diseases where fine control over a specific protein's abundance requires an RNA-based approach.

Your own project's gene and disease are what matter most, but to avoid falling behind, you still have to diligently keep an eye on what everyone else is doing.

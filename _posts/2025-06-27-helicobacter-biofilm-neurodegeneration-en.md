From mouth to anus, our body is a cave held at body temperature and continuously supplied with the right water and nutrients. Naturally, an enormous number of microbes live there. To avoid being swept away by the sudden flow of food, they secrete material and build a firm film — a biofilm — to stick to. If you are curious, run your tongue over unbrushed teeth. That sort of thing fills the tract from the stomach wall to the rectum.

Helicobacter infects more than 50% of humans. It enjoys unusually high name recognition in Korea thanks to Korea Yakult. For these bacteria to grow they must evict the existing tenants already living on the wall. The known route was that Helicobacter manipulates the infected gastric wall cells to release hormones that upset the gut environment for those bacteria. Yet the bacteria still cling on via biofilm. Helicobacter's next move for evicting the tenants is demolition of the biofilm.

## CagA, potent even at low concentration

Helicobacter secretes a protein called CagA which, at nanomolar (10^-9) levels, efficiently degrades the main biofilm proteins of other bacteria (FapC, CsgA). That washes the competing bacteria away, and Helicobacter settles into the space.

What makes this interesting is that a bacterial biofilm is one or two kinds of protein tangling together into a large, sticky mass — an amyloid — and amyloids of just this sort appear throughout neurodegenerative disease. There is a possibility it also acts on Abeta and tau in Alzheimer's, alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's, and so on. Testing it — in a lab setting, with protein alone, of course — showed the effect of blocking aggregation of all these proteins at nanomolar levels.

None of which means you need to go and catch Helicobacter. But a new candidate general-purpose inhibitor protein, one that works at low concentration, is an interesting subject.

## Three-line summary

1. The protein (CagA) Helicobacter uses to interfere with other bacteria's biofilm and drive them out works well even at very low concentrations.
2. Biofilm and the proteins suspected of causing neurodegenerative disease aggregate in similar ways.
3. CagA really can block the aggregation of neurodegenerative disease proteins — used well, might it serve as a therapeutic?

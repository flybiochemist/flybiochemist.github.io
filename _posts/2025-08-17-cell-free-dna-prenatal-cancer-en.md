If you have a baby, your OB probably suggested a NIPT test at some point. NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing) doesn't collect cells directly from the embryo or amniotic fluid — instead it detects the baby's DNA floating in the mother's blood, checking for chromosome-count abnormalities or specific gene defects. Getting cells directly from amniotic fluid is somewhat more accurate, but the non-invasive method is unambiguously safer, which is why it's drawn so much interest.

Back in the sequencing-poor 1990s, the first tests checked for a boy's Y chromosome (mothers don't have one) and, later, for chromosome-count abnormalities (deletions or duplications). Resolution has now climbed high enough to catch genes tied to developmental disorders too. Dennis Lo, who first pioneered this discovery, won the Lasker Prize for it in 2022.

## The same principle applies to cancer cells

But cell-free DNA isn't a phenomenon unique to mother and baby. Cancer cells inevitably carry mutations and chromosome-count abnormalities that differ substantially from the body's own genome, and they shed DNA into circulation frequently too — which raised the idea that analyzing this extracellular DNA might catch a major cancer before it grows large enough to see. Both fields have advanced together over the past decade or so, as next-generation sequencing got better at detecting tiny amounts of DNA while its cost dropped a thousandfold. Even so, reliably obtaining enough cell-free DNA remains genuinely hard.

## Platelets, it turns out, were storing DNA

The reason for that long preamble: a recent Science paper reports that platelets — known mainly for their role in blood clotting — actually store extracellular DNA and release it again when stimulated. Under experimental conditions, they scooped up loose circulating DNA in under six minutes. A function of platelets nobody knew about.

We still don't really know "why" platelets pick this DNA up. One plausible guess: extracellular DNA floating in blood is exactly the kind of thing that triggers inflammation (it resembles viral or bacterial DNA/RNA fragments, or the disordered DNA released by a dying cell, and can activate inflammatory signaling pathways), so quickly clearing it out may simply be housekeeping.

Which means we need to collect platelets carefully, without losing them, when running a liquid biopsy. Even in pancreatic cancer, where diagnosis so often comes too late, concentrating platelets properly in a mouse model reportedly allowed meaningfully earlier detection.

So here we have advances in sequencing technology, plus a use case matured through prenatal testing, now contributing to early cancer detection too. That's the strange charm of science.

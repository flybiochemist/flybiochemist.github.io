Today's post is prompted by something I read from a researcher who seems to study dementia from the exercise-science angle.

A few days ago I looked at a paper from a friend in the field. They expressed each of dementia's two culprit proteins separately in the fly brain. One (Abeta) causes trouble with protein production and quality control inside the brain. The other (tau), though, accelerated aging in parts of the body well outside the brain — the gut, the reproductive organs, adipose tissue (which doubles as the insect liver). Dementia only touches the brain, you say? Obviously not.

Not to the degree of cancer cachexia, but neurodegenerative disease patients still show more sarcopenia than normal aging would predict. Function and volume decline broadly across tissues beyond muscle too. And those declining tissues feed back negatively onto the brain.

## The signals muscle and brain exchange

That people who exercise a lot, and people with more muscle mass, get dementia less often has already been settled by population data. So work breaking down exactly what changes in the body when you exercise, molecule by molecule, has actually been going on for longer than you'd expect.

Small secreted proteins called hepatokines (made in the liver) and myokines (made in muscle) travel through the bloodstream to other organs, the brain among them. There, they act on neurons and glia, helping to keep those cells functioning well and healthy.

The best-studied of these is FNDC5 (the gene/precursor) and Irisin (the cleaved, active protein) — a single protein that has already shown strong beneficial effects in both dementia and Parkinson's on its own. Conversely, the muscle loss of old age comes with a drop in myokines. From there a vicious cycle can start: less muscle, worse disease, even less muscle.

## Fat and other organs are talking to the brain too

Less well studied than muscle, but other organs also exchange feedback with the brain about aging and health. Abdominal fat, as you'd guess, correlates badly with neurodegenerative disease — the more there is, the worse. Fat secretes adipokines too, and the more abdominal fat there is, the more those fat cells are stressed through interaction with immune cells, and stressed fat cells naturally change what their lipokines contain and how they're composed. And those go to the brain as well. Doesn't sound great for brain disease. The same kind of thing happens in a fatty liver, in the gut, in the kidney — across essentially every organ.

So how does the "dementia-driven accelerated aging" in the title actually happen? Honestly, we don't know yet. It shows up in flies, and something similar shows up in the gut in mice. Seeing it clearly in humans is hard, since everything is mixed together.

## Where this goes next

So future work will look at what a dementia-affected brain releases that drives this accelerated aging. And much as we now know specific proteins released by cancer cells worsen cachexia, and can suppress the symptom with an antibody therapy, we'll probably find ways to block whatever changes happen in neurodegenerative disease patients — or during aging more broadly.

Watching blood-pressure and energy-metabolism drugs quietly deliver on the dream of extending lifespan, the miracle cure really might already be close at hand: eat less, eat a varied diet, sleep well, keep your gut moving, exercise. Simple enough. Not that I manage a single one of them.

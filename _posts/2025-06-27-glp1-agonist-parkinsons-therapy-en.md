GLP-1 agonists, familiar to us as Ozempic and Wegovy, the weight-loss drugs that suppress appetite, are in numerous clinical trials that go beyond cardiovascular and kidney disease into neurodegeneration. There are several GLP-1 agonists; the front-runner, exenatide, was dropped in February after a phase 3 trial in Parkinson's showed no improvement. But other GLP-1 agonists — lixisenatide, and compounds in the liraglutide and semaglutide families — are spread across phases 1 to 3.

The research did not actually begin from knowing how it works in Parkinson's. The first place to show a positive effect in a Parkinson's mouse model was the NIA, the National Institute on Aging under the NIH, in 2009.

## From early trials to a brain-inflammation mechanism

In those mouse experiments, treatment with a GLP-1 agonist produced recovery in hypoxia-treated cells and in Parkinson's mouse models, and the effect vanished when the receptor was removed so GLP-1 could no longer act.

Then in 2013 a small trial with the GLP-1 agonist exenatide produced early data suggesting it slowed Parkinson's progression to some degree. Amusingly, a common side effect is weight loss.

The explanation for why this works is a bit thin, but one possibility is the easing of inflammation in the brain. A 2018 Nature Medicine paper from Ted Dawson's lab at Johns Hopkins, together with the labs of Seulki Lee and Han Seok Ko at the same institution, studied the mechanism in terms of relationships between cells in the brain.

When alpha-synuclein aggregates, clumped inside cells in Parkinson's, are poured into a normal mouse brain, microglia — which handle immunity — and astrocytes, which normally regulate neuronal homeostasis, come under stress and shift into disease-activated astrocytes that no longer support neurons, worsening the condition. A GLP-1 agonist suppresses this.

Other papers describe further effects: improved mitochondrial function within cells, activation of new neuronal regeneration, and more.

Parkinson's still has some way to go before joining the GLP-1-cures-everything story. And rather than the semaglutide or liraglutide people take for obesity, we will probably need a differently shaped peptide that crosses the barrier around the brain well. But the possibility is always alive.

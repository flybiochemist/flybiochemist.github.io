A review from everyone's favorite (and most argued-about) aging scientist, Tony Wyss-Coray: biological aging clocks in health and disease.

There comes an age when you're just not "what you used to be" — when you feel yourself starting to decline. The moment you notice it, you've officially crossed over. But turning that feeling into a scientifically grounded threshold is not easy. So aging scientists put together a composite picture: chromatin winding and unwinding, changes in gene expression, shifts in the proteins and lipids circulating in blood and other body fluids, immune activation, and the functional decline of muscles and organs — all folded into a single definition of aging and its trajectory.

Most of this work has been done in mice first, and cross-species differences are a given, so applying mouse findings to humans always calls for caution.

## Humans don't age linearly

According to papers from Wyss-Coray's group and their collaborators, blood protein profiles pivot sharply around ages 34 and 60; a combined analysis of blood lipids, metabolites, immune cells, and the gut microbiome points to 43 and 60 as key inflection points. The brain is hard to sample directly, so it's usually measured by volume instead, and here the proposed turning points are all over the place — 57, 70, 78; 40 and 70-75; 9, 32, 66, 83. Based on this kind of data, the author pins the scientifically meaningful "turning points" at 30-40, 60-70, and 80-plus. In other words, humans don't age in a straight line — we suddenly age a lot at certain points, then hold roughly steady for a while.

## Three ways to measure aging

So how do you actually measure aging and life expectancy? Probably through some combination of markers. This review leans on three approaches to find candidate markers:

1. DNA methylation — methylation controls how tightly the histone "spool" winds or unwinds DNA, which in turn controls whether a gene gets expressed. Too much winding or unwinding in the wrong place, and expression goes haywire.
2. Blood protein changes — tracking which circulating proteins rise or fall with age, especially ones tied to the aging of specific organs.
3. Organ aging itself — measured by volume, or by rising and falling metabolites tied to that organ's function. This one is tightly linked to the second — it's where imaging and metabolomics have to meet.

All of this gets tracked longitudinally as people age, in the hope of nailing down aging more precisely.

## Every organ runs its own clock

Since each organ seems to run its own aging clock, people whose organs have broken down across the board should, in principle, age faster and face higher risk overall. And for any given disease, certain organs or cell types matter more than others. For dementia, for instance, the authors suspect astrocytes — the glial cells, more than the neurons themselves — carry a more direct link to aging. (Maybe that's why GFAP, an astrocyte marker, keeps showing up in dementia-related spinal fluid and blood tests.) Still, having a clearer sense of which markers actually matter is progress.

In animal studies, anti-aging effects showed up for the brain specifically with exercise, vitamin D and omega-3s, and — no surprise these days — GLP-1 agonists, famous from Wegovy. (Opinions on this vary by research group, and none of this is human data.) Bottom line: there's still no miracle drug for eternal youth. Eat well, exercise, and try not to stress too much.

*This is outside my field, so there may be errors.*

As I have written before, the synapse — the connection between two neurons — is strengthened by differences in signalling intensity, or, when no signal passes, quietly pruned away.

This connecting and disconnecting used to be explained mainly as an interaction between neurons, but the role of the surrounding cells that regulate it is drawing more and more attention. I have previously written about astrocytes and their ability to regulate memory retrieval in neurons. Today's is a different glial cell: microglia, which handle immune function in the brain and are known to prune synapses — and how they go about removing a fear memory.

## Microglia that eat synapses

Synapse formation and subsequent pruning during development are very well described. Synapses packed densely up to age one are pruned appropriately as we get older. (Too little pruning gives schizophrenia, too much gives autism, so trimming down to the right connections is essential.) Microglia do the removing, by eating the synapses. But after development, in the adult, what role do microglia play in forming and erasing memory? This paper uses fear memory as the tool.

Mice are given a fearful situation — an electric shock paired with a conditioned stimulus — and newly activated neurons are identified with c-fos. These are engram neurons in a specific part of the hippocampus, the dentate gyrus. Curiously, fear memory is maintained largely by neurons in this one region.

A fear-generated memory fades over time. You can read this out by re-presenting the conditioned stimulus that accompanied the shock and seeing whether the animal freezes.

At the cellular level, looking around the neurons newly activated by the fearful environment — presumably the ones carrying the fear memory — microglia are found attached. Three-dimensional reconstructions from stacks of micrographs show microglia munching on synapses. And the microglia that go to these engram neurons do so in relation to the neuron's own activation. In other words, they attach to activated neurons and their synapses and slowly reduce that neuron's connectivity.

## Suppress microglia and the memory lasts

So would weakening microglial function make the fear memory persist? The authors track fear memory in a mouse model with microglia selectively suppressed. As expected, it takes longer to fade. They then verify this by other routes.

When pruning synapses during development, microglia coat a synapse with complement proteins to mark it as one to be removed, recognise that mark through a receptor, and eat it. Here the authors express a complement-inhibiting protein in neurons. Again synapse removal slows and the fear memory declines more slowly.

Earlier we said microglia arrive having caught the scent of neuronal activity. Now the authors suppress the activity of the newly formed fear-memory neurons immediately after learning. The microglia fail to arrive, so the synapses are not eaten, the connections are preserved, and the fear neurons hold onto the memory well once reactivated.

Where earlier work on engram neurons and microglia mostly explained things through inflammation and the exchange of molecules, what makes this paper fun is the physical explanation. I look forward to applications in synaptic regulation and in erasing fear memories.

My own hunch: with microglia and astrocytes both covered, we will now see plenty of papers on oligodendrocytes in memory and other neuronal functions. Which means more neuron-glia co-culture (primary cells or organoids), and more experiments in mice using Cre lines and viruses that allow neuron- and glia-specific manipulation.

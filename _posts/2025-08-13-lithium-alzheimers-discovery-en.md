You've probably seen plenty of "Lithium treats Alzheimer's!" headlines lately, on the news and in your Threads feed. A recent Nature paper is behind the buzz. Lithium, which most of us know only from batteries — why would it have anything to do with treating Alzheimer's, and why has nobody run this obvious-sounding experiment before now? The answer: they had, it hadn't worked, and this time a new angle came to light.

Lithium has long been used as a drug for various brain disorders, and it was already well known that Alzheimer's rates were lower among people taking it. Lithium reduces the activity of two proteins, GSK3beta and CDK5, which are the primary kinases phosphorylating tau, one of Alzheimer's key proteins — phosphorylated tau begins clumping on its own, and that produces the intracellular tau tangles that are one of Alzheimer's pathological hallmarks. Less kinase activity means less tau clumping.

## A drug people were excited about 20 years ago, but...

Lithium also enhances autophagy, one of the cell's own cleanup mechanisms. Between these two functions — dephosphorylating tau so it clumps less, and improving the machinery that breaks down whatever has already clumped — it's no accident that lithium kept turning up in every kind of cell-level screen for tau-modulating candidates. Twenty years ago, people already expected lithium to treat Alzheimer's.

But while it showed some effect in mouse models overexpressing tau or APP (the precursor to Abeta, Alzheimer's other culprit protein), lithium given to actual Alzheimer's patients in clinical trials showed no meaningful benefit. And it quietly faded from view.

## Lithium in the brain was trapped in the wrong place

What makes this new Nature paper clever is its opening. It starts from the very contradiction that sank the old theory — lithium shows little effect in a living body, yet patients on lithium have lower Alzheimer's rates — and points out something earlier papers had missed: a broad, and locally specific, lithium deficiency in the brain, and its relationship to Alzheimer's.

The paper shows that from mild cognitive impairment through full Alzheimer's, the brain carries less lithium overall than a normal brain — while lithium is present at high concentration specifically within amyloid plaques (the extracellular protein clumps made of Abeta, one of Alzheimer's two hallmark lesions). In other words, as Alzheimer's progresses, lithium ends up trapped in the extracellular protein clumps instead of staying where it's actually needed, inside the cell.

To reproduce this sequestration-driven intracellular lithium deficiency, the team fed Alzheimer's model mice a lithium-depleted diet. The model mice developed Alzheimer's symptoms earlier — tau phosphorylation, Abeta tangles, memory decline, loss of synapses at neuron terminals, brain inflammation. Expression of ApoE, the major Alzheimer's risk gene mentioned before, shot right up too. All from simply removing lithium from the diet.

So what went wrong with the earlier dietary-lithium experiments?

## The form mattered

Reactive lithium exists bound to various compounds. The forms we encounter most — lithium chloride (bound to chlorine) and lithium carbonate (bound to carbonate) — are what earlier experiments mostly used in animal models and clinical trials, and it turns out these happened to be exactly the forms that readily deposit into Abeta clumps.

So would a compound-bound form less prone to that deposition produce a bigger effect by actually restoring the brain's lithium shortfall? The team gave lithium bound to orotate, the form that deposits least, and the major disease markers (Abeta, tau) dropped by 50% in animals that combined lithium deficiency and Alzheimer's pathology. In other words: the earlier failures came from using only the form of lithium that Alzheimer's pathology itself kidnaps, and lithium bound to an organic salt should actually help.

By reconciling two theories that had been fighting each other, this paper made it into Nature. And organic-salt lithium is extremely cheap on top of that. Here's hoping for good results in the clinic going forward.

## Three-line summary

1. People taking lithium therapeutically get dementia less often, yet feeding lithium to animals or patients in trials never showed much benefit.
2. Lithium appears to get caught and deposited in patients' extracellular Abeta plaques, starving the cells that actually need it and causing a cascade of problems.
3. An organic-salt form of lithium that deposits less into plaque looks like it could help replenish the lithium shortage in a dementia patient's brain.

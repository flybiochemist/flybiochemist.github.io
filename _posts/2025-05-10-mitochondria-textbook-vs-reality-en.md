Biology class teaches us about the mitochondrion: the organelle responsible for producing cellular energy from glucose and oxygen, which also stores calcium ions for signalling, and which, during development or when damage is severe enough that a cell must die, triggers the orderly form of cell death — apoptosis — that spares neighbouring cells the collateral damage.

The shape we see in textbooks, round with a squiggly double membrane inside, comes from electron microscopy of intracellular organelles in the 1950s. Inside a roundish membrane sat a characteristic double membrane, and once biochemists of every stripe piled in, they found that ATP synthesis happens on that inner membrane. Which is why we so often see the mitochondrion as a cross-section in a textbook. But a textbook diagram is a schematic — it does not carry accurate information the way a subway map does.

## In reality it is more like a mesh

In most cells, mitochondria are simply everywhere — about 25% of cell volume. And the number usually quoted, 100 to 2,000, is a count based on mitochondrial DNA; in reality they are mostly all connected. So under a microscope, mitochondria look less like the kidney-bean shape in the textbook and more like a mesh. To reflect reality, there should be more of them than the two or three drawn in a diagram, and they should be more connected.

The worse a cell's condition, the more it tends to snap off just the damaged part from this network and discard it as a separate, broken-off mitochondrion. The presence of such fragmented mitochondria is linked to many diseases; in Parkinson's, one suspected cause is that when the signalling that tags and breaks down damaged mitochondria on the outer membrane — Parkin-Pink1-mediated mitophagy — fails to work, the broken mitochondria are not cleared in time.

## The bean shape has its reason to exist, too

Does that mean every short, standalone, pill-shaped mitochondrion of the kind we see in textbooks, disconnected from the network, is a sign of disease? No. That bean shape is actually useful when a mitochondrion needs to be transported somewhere.

Neurons constantly fire electrical signals via calcium-sodium pumps and run a cycle of releasing, receiving and recycling neurotransmitter at their terminals, so they are enormously energy-hungry tissue — and their structure is far too long and complicated to make energy centrally and ship it to the periphery. So rather than generating energy centrally and sending it to the terminal, it is more efficient to send mitochondria to where the energy is used and make it there. Dynein and kinesin walk diligently along microtubules, carrying mitochondria on their backs all the way to the terminal. Which is why these particular mitochondria look more like the textbook bean than the mesh.

Anyway, a textbook is like a subway map. It is not the real world.

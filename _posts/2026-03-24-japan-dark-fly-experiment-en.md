On November 11, 1954, Dr. Syuichi Mori began an experiment: raise a line of fruit flies entirely in the dark for their whole lives, generation after generation, and see what changes.

For a proper control, Dr. Mori split the same fly lineage's descendants in half — one half raised in constant darkness, the other on a normal 12-hour light/dark cycle. Unfortunately the light/dark control group was later lost entirely when an incubator malfunctioned and cooked them all, so the current control is a wild-type line with some drift of its own.

Dr. Mori died in 2007, but the experiment continued. With sequencing technology now advanced enough, a 2016 study identified the mutations present in flies raised in the dark for 61 years and 1,500 generations. But it was hard to know whether these were mutations that had simply drifted to fixation through continuous inbreeding, or ones actually useful for surviving in the dark. So a Japanese team designed the following experiment.

## Keeping only the necessary genes via a hybrid cross

Some genes in the dark flies presumably really are better suited to survival without light. So the hypothesis: cross them with normal flies, put the offspring back in the dark, and after several generations, only the genes truly necessary for surviving in darkness should remain.

They crossed dark flies with normal flies and raised the offspring in darkness for roughly 50 generations. Then they compared the genetic variants present in the flies that survived in the dark against the variants found in the original dark-fly line. Around 100 variants, mostly related to olfaction, showed up in both the dark line and this hybrid population.

The experiment is still ongoing. This is Japanese science.

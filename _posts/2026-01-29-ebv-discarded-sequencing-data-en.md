What separates people vulnerable to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) from people who shrug it off?

In an experiment, we see what we're looking for and discard the rest. In a Western blot, the band at the size we want; in IF, the signal we're expecting. When something odd shows up, we run the experiment again and pick the one that came out clean. Population geneticists have apparently done the same thing for a long time. People inevitably carry infections from various viruses, so viral sequence contaminating human genome sequencing data has traditionally been stripped out before analysis. One group had the opposite idea.

1. Viral DNA keeps showing up. So do people more susceptible to a virus — those with more viral DNA than others — happen to carry a different set of genes?

2. These days, connections between viruses and disease are a live question. So what actual diseases do people with a lot of detectable viral DNA have?

## 90% were infected

Using UK Biobank and All of Us, the two great troves of population genomic data, researchers found that roughly 90% of people carry Epstein-Barr virus. Among those, they compared people whose EBV sits latent against those whose EBV is active (and whose viral DNA levels are elevated as a result).

The single biggest factor separating latent from active turned out to be how well someone's body makes antibodies against EBV. About 10% of people made relatively few antibodies (low antibody titer in blood), and in this group EBV tended to be active. And these same people carried variants across 22 immune-related genes. The study couldn't show that this active virus directly causes disease, but it did find a connection to autoimmune disease.

So depending on the question you bring to it, someone else's discarded data can yield a genuinely good result.

The most interesting thing about a seminar I attended recently was not the content itself but a particular situation: what happens when a frameshift mutation occurs in a GC-rich region.

The blueprint that is DNA becomes a functional element — protein — by way of an RNA copy. The blueprint is base-4 (ATGC), and since proteins are usually built from 20 amino acids, letting three letters of DNA-RNA specify one amino acid gives 64 slots, enough for every amino acid plus start and stop signals.

The moment one or two bases are inserted into or deleted from this three-letter code, a frameshift occurs — like slipping a row on an answer sheet — and every amino acid after that point is garbage.

## GC-rich regions are the problem

Usually the shifted reading runs into a stop codon at some point and produces only a truncated protein (TAA, TAG, TGA in DNA). The problem is regions rich in GC.

In an ordinary frameshift, a few to a few dozen unwanted amino acids get made before the newly created stop codon ends translation. The resulting abnormal short protein is quickly routed for degradation by the cell's various systems for detecting faulty proteins, and homeostasis holds.

Regions of repeating GC have few of the A and T bases a stop codon requires, so termination does not readily happen. What gets produced is a protein long enough and strange enough that the cell's first-pass inspection for defective proteins cannot filter it out. Such proteins may compete with the originals, travel somewhere they do not belong and act there, tangle with each other and overload the degradation machinery, or even acquire an entirely new function. From an evolutionary standpoint that might be fine — but only for the ones that survived. In genetic disease and cancer it can only be an unlucky case.

Even having a mutation land in a good spot is a kind of luck.

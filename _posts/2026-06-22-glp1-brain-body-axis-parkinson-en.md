GLP-1 agonists such as Wegovy and Mounjaro, used in diabetes and obesity, have been probed as Parkinson's treatments for the past decade. They worked well in mouse experiments. In humans there is a trend, but phase 3 trials keep failing.

The paper I want to introduce today explains that gap through expression levels of the receptor that binds the drug and carries the signal from the cell surface inward. Patients were split into a group with above-average receptor expression (high GRS) and a below-average group (low GRS), and Parkinson's incidence under treatment was calculated for each. A significant difference emerged between the groups over time — the higher the receptor expression, the less Parkinson's appeared. In Alzheimer's and related conditions, though, the difference in effect was limited.

The authors suspect the reason is that the body-brain axis matters more in Parkinson's. As I have mentioned before, one leading mechanism for Parkinson's is that alpha-synuclein aggregates formed in peripheral organs — mainly the gut, and lately the kidney and liver as well — travel to the brain along the vagus nerve. So perhaps GLP-1 agonists clear these more effectively at each of those peripheral sites.

At any rate, for those of you on GLP-1 who happen to carry plenty of the receptor that makes the drug work: I am about 1.5 times envious.

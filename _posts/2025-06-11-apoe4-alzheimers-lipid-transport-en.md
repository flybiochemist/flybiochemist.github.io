The gene I've been citing most while writing my third paper, and one of the hottest topics in Alzheimer's research, is the E4 type of the APOE gene.

Alzheimer's patients show two hallmark features: aggregated amyloid-beta outside cells and aggregated tau protein inside them. Most patients show both, yet mutation carriers in the relevant genes are surprisingly rare. Early-onset dementia patients with mutations in APP (Abeta's precursor) or in the beta- and gamma-secretases that cut it into Abeta make up only a tiny fraction of cases (1-5%). The rest happens probabilistically, through combinations of various risk genes. And the most famous of those risk genes is APOE.

The APOE gene comes in three types — E2, E3, and E4 — differing by three sequence positions, and inheriting E4 from both parents carries the highest known risk.

## Risk differs by ethnicity

Population-based studies suggest the E4 form is more common in Europeans than in Africans or Hispanics, and more common still in East Asians than in Europeans, and that the actual conversion rate to Alzheimer's is higher in East Asians than in other groups (this needs more research). What is certain is that this genotype raises dementia risk in every population studied.

## What does APOE actually do?

So what is APOE, exactly? A fat transporter. Think of it as something that grips fat (and phospholipids) tightly while moving it from one cell, or one tissue, to another. Even the LDL cholesterol that shows up on a blood test isn't floating around on its own — fat-binding proteins like this one carry it. In the brain it is expressed mainly by astrocytes, the cells that support neurons — not by the neurons that die in dementia. So how does APOE4 made in astrocytes raise the odds of dementia?

A brief detour into what makes neurons special: neurons are extraordinarily unusual cells. Unlike most cells, once differentiated they do not die for an extremely long time — essentially a lifetime. Compare that to gut cells (2-3 days) or cardiac muscle cells, the next longest-lived at 20-30 years. Neurons are also the longest cells in the body and among the most energy-hungry.

Because of that, rather than making or breaking down what they need themselves, neurons tend to hand the job off to astrocytes. Every day, neurons receive fresh supplies — glucose or lactate for energy, phospholipids for the membrane — and release signalling molecules and waste that the glia take back, process, and return. This back-and-forth transport between neuron and glia is essential to neuronal health.

The leading hypothesis right now is that two amino acid changes alter APOE4's fat-transport capacity, disrupting this exchange and causing a cascade of problems.

## When fat accumulates, cleanup stops

APOE4-carrying mouse models often show lipid droplets forming in astrocytes — the same thing that appears in liver cells in fatty liver disease. Make what you need, fail to transport it, and it piles up. APOE4 is also reportedly better at carrying oxidised fat and worse at carrying ordinary fat. The same thing happens in microglia, the brain's other glial cell. Microglia are supposed to clear amyloid-beta floating outside cells, but in glia loaded with fat, that function declines.

Fat accumulation impairs glial function, impaired glia fail to clear amyloid-beta, the accumulating amyloid-beta drives inflammation in neurons and glia alike, the resulting stress pushes neurons to pump out even more amyloid-beta, and the brain is now in a vicious cycle...

There is still no settled treatment specific to genotype. For now, all we can do is be more careful.

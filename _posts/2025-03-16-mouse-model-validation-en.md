The first paper to come out of my current lab was about making a Parkinson's model mouse. The paper is out, but for public distribution there was a problem: since I ran every experiment myself, there had been no cross-validation.

So a postdoc who recently joined our team is now using the new mouse line in their own experiments as a cross-check. The earliest-appearing phenotypes have reproduced well so far. All the phenotypes should be validated within about three months.

But neither the new postdoc nor I can run a fully blind experiment. We have raised these mice from birth, and even though the labels have been swapped for random numbers, a tiny detail is enough to let us roughly "guess" whether a given mouse is the Parkinson's line. So we are moving to a double-blind design.

An outside lab has also asked to take the model and analyse it, and I plan to give them nothing but cage numbers and serial IDs. If it holds up there too, I will be able to relax a little.

## Mouse lines that spread too soon and caused headaches

There is, in fact, a history of mouse lines being released to the public too early and causing trouble.

In the Alzheimer's field, the 3xTG line, which induces both of Alzheimer's two hallmark features (Abeta plaque and tau tangle), developed a problem in one gene when it arrived in Korea, and several labs could not reproduce results until a properly maintained line came in.

I hear the recent Abeta knock-in line (APP-NL-F) also had reproducibility problems in its early, not-yet-stabilised form and had to be hastily re-distributed later. (This is just something I heard, so take it with a grain of salt.)

rTG4510, a line that dominated the Alzheimer's field for a period, happened to disrupt five genes at once (a five-gene knockout), and once some of its phenotypes turned out to be caused by those gene disruptions rather than the intended pathology, it had no choice but to step down from the throne.

I will validate my own mouse as thoroughly as I can before sending it out, but there could still be a flaw I do not know about. Even so, giving whoever takes it the most accurate information possible is the author's responsibility.

## A postscript on diagnosing Parkinson's from cerebrospinal fluid

In the brain, too, a large share of Parkinson's patients — I do not remember the exact number, something like 70% — show the Lewy body, a cellular aggregate of lipid and protein, in which a hyperphosphorylated form of alpha-synuclein is detected. Drawing cerebrospinal fluid, mixing it with alpha-synuclein protein, and watching how quickly the alpha-synuclein aggregates has begun to be used as an early, if crude, tool for predicting Parkinson's. If you ask whether this has been proven, I can only say that it is, so far, the most likely candidate. Other schools of thought have other theories, of course, and are making their own case in part by leaving this one out. But nobody can walk every road at once. And at least in mouse models, this approach shows far better reproducibility than the alternatives.

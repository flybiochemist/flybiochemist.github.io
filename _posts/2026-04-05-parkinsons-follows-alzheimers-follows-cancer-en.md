My personal sense is that in molecular biology, some diseases lead the trends and others follow. Cancer biology is out furthest ahead, Alzheimer's trails one step behind, and Parkinson's trails Alzheimer's another step behind that.

Writing the discussion section of a paper, I will note something not yet tried in Parkinson's, go looking, and find that Alzheimer's has already covered about half of it. And if Alzheimer's has not covered it either, digging through the enormous cancer literature usually turns up a concept you can borrow.

It feels a bit like running a local restaurant that imports a dish from abroad and localises the seasoning slightly before serving it. But if it tastes good and sells, that is fine.

## The layers of knowledge a Parkinson's researcher needs

In that spirit, anyone doing molecular-biology-based Parkinson's research needs to keep reading the relevant literature and absorb the "cause and resolution" narrative as it plays out in cancer, Alzheimer's, and other neurodegenerative diseases. That much is baseline.

Next, since this happens inside the cell, you need to know the organelles well. Parkinson's has a lot of mitochondria- and lysosome-related genes, so you need to keep up with the latest papers on those organelles.

And this happens in the brain. The narrative in Parkinson's and parts of Alzheimer's is that aggregated protein spreads from one region to another, mostly travelling along the brain's connectivity. So you have to study which region connects to which.

But the brain's function differs by region, its neurons and glia differ, there are many types of neuron, and each disease affects them differently. So you need to know the properties of the relevant regions and cell types, and the overlaps and differences in which regions and cell types are affected across diseases.

## And an endlessly growing list

These days it is easier than it used to be to get the genes of people with the disease and their expression levels, and dozens of associated genes have turned up. You need to know something of the methodology behind how these newly added genes are found each year, and what the newly discovered genes actually do, in order to connect them to anything.

Add in changes across the metabolome — protein and lipid alike — and you also need some protein structure, some sense of how the chromosome carrying a given gene is regulated, and on top of that the craft of raising cells or mice well, handling them carefully, catching subtle differences, and generating data with consistency.

In the end it is a local restaurant, but one that has to stay current with the trends while still doing the basics properly. Every profession is like this to some degree, but by the time you are a PhD, the required volume of knowledge is considerable.

I wrote this myself and I am already tired of it.

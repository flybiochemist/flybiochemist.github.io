The attached figure is Parkinson's prevalence relative to the number of Medicare recipients (65+) across the US in 2012.

The prevalence in Florida and Southern California, where retirees cluster, makes some sense. But the southern belt from Texas to Florida, the heavily farmed regions of the central-eastern US, and the Rust Belt are also notably high compared with other regions.

Looking at this data and trying to work out what drives Parkinson's, you realise how complicated it is.

- Ethnicity: people of Asian descent have lower Parkinson's prevalence than Europeans
- Age: roughly 0.5% at 65, 5% at 85
- Environment: pesticides and urban particulate matter raise risk 1.5–3-fold
- Lifestyle: education, economic status, and regular exercise all lower it

Between the Rockies and the Mountain-Time-zone regions to their west, where potatoes are grown at real scale, farming is plenty intensive, yet Parkinson's incidence is low. Perhaps the pesticides used on soy and corn are simply much harsher.

What I want to read together today is an Alzforum piece, 'Rare Variants Offer Clues to APOE Toxicity: Lipidation, Aggregation'. A little background for anyone unfamiliar with ApoE.

The article covers not published papers but four BioRxiv preprints — still in peer review. That several papers likely headed for major journals within a year or two are piled onto a single gene tells you how hot the topic is.

## Three types that set Alzheimer's risk

ApoE is a protein involved in transporting cholesterol. Humans mostly carry one of three types. E4 is the ancestral form; add two sequence variants and you get E3, then E2. Calling them mutations makes E3 and E2 sound like the bad outcome, but in Alzheimer's the more of those variants you carry, the better off you are. One copy of E4 raises Alzheimer's risk three- to fourfold relative to the other variants; two copies, ten- to fifteenfold. And E4 is common compared with other mutations. Hence the intense interest in ApoE and Alzheimer's.

The second reason ApoE drew attention is the Christchurch mutation (R136S). In a Colombian family carrying an early-onset dementia gene (a Psen mutation causing Abeta overproduction), where immediate relatives all developed Alzheimer's in their forties, one woman remained sharp and dementia-free into her seventies. Sequencing her revealed a mutation at position 136, outside the usual E2/E3/E4 sites (112, 158), and this became the suspected source of the strong protection.

Mouse models later confirmed the protective effect of the Christchurch mutation. From there, one line of work asked what other mutations ApoE might harbour, another asked how such mutations raise or lower Alzheimer's risk. Which is how four papers landed in a single month.

## Aggregation and lipidation, the newly described mechanisms

What these preprints add is new Alzheimer's-associated variants in ApoE and, more importantly, mechanism: aggregation between ApoE molecules, and excessive delivery of lipids such as cholesterol by ApoE to microglia.

Among seven new variants found by comparison against All of Us data, W276C lowered risk to roughly two-thirds, while R189C tripled it. ApoE aggregation runs in the same direction. Variants already known to be protective (E3, W276C, V236E, R251G) showed less ApoE-to-ApoE aggregation than E4 or the unmutated form. Why that aggregation causes disease is not yet fully answered, but one can at least entertain the idea that it is where the disease begins.

One of the biggest differences between E4 and E3/E2 lies in ApoE's actual job, cholesterol transport. About 30% of ApoE4 particles were lipid-bound, against roughly 20% for ApoE3 and ApoE2. The risk variant R189C pushed ApoE3's lipidation up to ApoE4 levels; on the protective side there was either no difference (V236E and W276C) or, for R251G, a drop of ApoE4's lipidation down to ApoE3 levels.

Lipid-bound ApoE secreted by neurons then delivers more lipid to microglia, which handle immunity in the brain, and to astrocytes, which maintain homeostasis and carry out most lipid-related metabolism. The cause is still not certain — my own guess is a broad impairment of degradation from lysosomal dysfunction — but glia that take up a lot of this lipid shift toward an inflammatory state.

The further this work goes on how ApoE variants protect against or predispose to Alzheimer's, the more it will contribute to treating Alzheimer's, and some of Parkinson's too.

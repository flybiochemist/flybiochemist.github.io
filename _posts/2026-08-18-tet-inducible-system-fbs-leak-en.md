Tet-inducible systems let you switch a gene on or off in cells whenever you like. The most common version is the tet-inducible system: tetracycline, or its analog doxycycline, either blocks a promoter-bound protein and shuts down transcription (tet-off), or, in the reverse design, keeps that protein from binding the promoter at all unless the drug is present (tet-on). Either way, the drug changes the shape of the protein sitting on the promoter, and that's what flips transcription on or off.

Here's the catch. The single most essential ingredient for growing cells is fetal bovine serum (FBS) — blood plasma harvested from calves still in utero. If the mother cow ever got antibiotics, that plasma carries some residual tetracycline. So unless you specifically buy tet-free FBS, there's always a trace amount of tetracycline sitting in your dish, and your gene is never fully on or fully off.

Keep an eye on anything in the tet family.

In neurodegenerative disease, by the time symptoms appear the pathology is already well advanced. If early diagnosis were possible, more treatment options would be open — an antibody therapy, or a drug that dampens inflammation in the brain, applied in advance. But how would you diagnose early?

In Parkinson's, protein aggregates called Lewy bodies are found at the lesion site, and alpha-synuclein is one of the main aggregating proteins in them. So the idea is that measuring the amount of aggregated alpha-synuclein in a patient's brain might allow early diagnosis, and two approaches are under study.

## Two approaches: SAA and PET

SAA (seed amplification assay) exploits the way an alpha-synuclein aggregate pulls in normal protein, makes it part of itself, and amplifies its characteristic structure. A small amount of seeding aggregate from cerebrospinal fluid is mixed with normal protein, and how quickly the aggregate grows is measured to predict the likelihood of disease.

The other method introduces a tracer that inserts itself specifically into alpha-synuclein aggregates, then images with PET (positron emission tomography) and analyses the result. It shows both the distribution and the degree of aggregate formation.

Both are of course still being optimised. For SAA, which relies on amplification, it would be good to work from blood or saliva rather than the awkward business of cerebrospinal fluid or a skin biopsy — but that is not easy. I hear the batch effects are still severe, both in extracting aggregates from such samples without altering them and in running the amplification. For PET tracers, finding one that is both stable and disease-specific is very hard. It also has to cross the blood-brain barrier and cell membranes well.

Still, just as blood-level antibody testing and PET imaging have advanced a great deal in Alzheimer's, there will be good results in Parkinson's someday. And the company that makes a good tracer will do very well.
